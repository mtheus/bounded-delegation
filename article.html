Bounded Delegation: Dividing Context and Decision Between Humans and Coding Agents

A comparative review of agent-oriented development techniques and a research agenda for 2027

Matheus Bastos Neves
Independent Researcher
linkedin.com/in/matheusbn

November 13, 2026

Abstract

Context. Coding agents now write most of the implementation in many teams, and a market of techniques has emerged to tell them what a system is and what it should do: specification kits, multi-agent methods, review plugins, skill collections, knowledge-base formats, and code-as-specification workflows. Empirical evidence suggests that individual speed gains do not translate into team-level gains, and that AI shifts the dominant risk from technical debt to cognitive and intent debt.

Objective. We ask how current techniques structure and verify the knowledge agents need, which kinds of debt they leave unaddressed, and what division of reading and decision between humans and agents best preserves software health in iterative, incremental development.

Method. We conduct a multivocal review of eleven approaches (snapshot: November 2026) and compare them on nine criteria, including directory structure, verification, drift control, and the three debts of Storey’s model. We interpret the results through boundary object theory (Star and Griesemer, 1989; Carlile, 2002), building on a recent theoretical review of user stories (Sporsem et al., 2026).

Results. No reviewed technique maintains an explicit architecture model across features; cognitive debt is a blind spot of the market; and verification splits into two disconnected worlds, one checking that prose is clear and one checking that behaviour is correct.

Proposal. We formulate the Bounded Delegation hypothesis: delegating implementation to agents is sustainable when, and only when, it is bounded on one side by human-owned intent and on the other by human-readable executable evidence, with both bounds kept current by feedback. We derive a model with seven knowledge layers, three roles, and a feedback ring; six testable sub-hypotheses; an evaluation agenda; a contrast with the software engineering canon (Pressman, Sommerville, the SWEBOK, Brooks, and the authors of the Agile Manifesto) that separates the fundamentals preserved from those that change; and the process practices that lose their rationale, from GitFlow to release trains.

Keywords: coding agents; spec-driven development; software architecture; context engineering; cognitive debt; intent debt; human–AI collaboration; software process; boundary objects; agile requirements engineering.

Introduction

In 1985, Peter Naur argued that programming is theory building: the code is only the visible trace of an understanding held by the people who wrote it, and when that understanding is lost the program begins to die even if the code is intact (Naur, 1985). Coding agents make this observation urgent. An agent writes code fluently but starts every session without the theory of the system. Whatever is not written down, it infers, and it delivers something plausible, fast, and often wrong.

Storey (2026) names the cost. Beyond technical debt, which lives in code, teams accumulate cognitive debt, the erosion of shared understanding, and intent debt, the absence of externalised goals, constraints, and rationale that humans and agents can consult. Her central claim is that generative AI tends to reduce the first while accelerating the other two.

Industry responded quickly. Between 2025 and 2026 a market of techniques appeared, each placing the missing theory somewhere in the repository. They differ in the unit of knowledge they choose (a feature, a change, a capability, a phase, a code directory, a concept, a test), and that choice determines what an agent can know and what is lost over time. No shared framework exists for comparing them, and none states explicitly what humans must still read.

Research questions

  • RQ1. How do current techniques for agent-assisted development structure, verify, and maintain the knowledge that agents need?

  • RQ2. Which of the three debts (technical, cognitive, intent) does each technique address, and which gaps remain across the market?

  • RQ3. What division of reading and decision between humans and agents best preserves software health in iterative, incremental development?

  • RQ4. Measured against the software engineering canon, which fundamentals are preserved under that division, which change, and which established process practices lose their rationale?

RQ1 and RQ2 are answered by a comparative review (Sections 4–6). RQ3 and RQ4 are answered by a proposed model and its contrast with the software engineering canon (Sections 7–8), stated as hypotheses to be tested (Section 9).

Central hypothesis

The review leads to one organising claim, which also names the model.

The Bounded Delegation hypothesis (H0). Delegating implementation to coding agents is sustainable when, and only when, the delegation is bounded on both sides by artifacts that humans own: intent before the work (problem, domain language, decisions) and evidence after it (inspectable executable scenarios and their results). What lies between the bounds can be written, read, and maintained by agents, provided the bounds are verified independently of their authors and kept current by feedback.

“Sustainable” is used in the sense of the triple debt model: throughput is maintained or improved without a compounding increase in technical, cognitive, or intent debt. Section 7.1 decomposes H0 into six sub-hypotheses, each with a falsifiable prediction.

Contributions

  1. A comparison framework of nine criteria for agent-oriented development techniques.

  2. A multivocal review of eleven approaches, with strengths and weaknesses of each.

  3. An analysis of knowledge directory structures, identifying seven organising axes.

  4. The Bounded Delegation model: seven knowledge layers, three roles, a feedback ring, and an explicit statement of what humans read and what agents read.

  5. A contrast with the software engineering canon that identifies the fundamentals preserved and changed, and the process practices whose rationale weakens.

  6. A reading of the two bounds as boundary objects between people and agents, mapped to Carlile’s syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic knowledge boundaries, with agile requirements engineering as their nearest precedent.

  7. An evaluation agenda that operationalises the hypotheses.

Background

Theory building and the triple debt model

Naur’s thesis implies that the essential asset of a software team is not an artifact but an understanding. Storey (2026) turns this into a diagnostic model with three layers of a software system (goals and intent, code and structure, shared understanding) and a debt for each. Technical debt lives in code and limits how the system can change. Cognitive debt lives in people and limits how a team can reason about change. Intent debt lives in artifacts and limits whether the system keeps reflecting what was meant. The debts reinforce one another: poorly recorded intent prevents accurate mental models; teams that do not understand a system make poor implementation choices; and unhealthy code erodes what understanding remains.

Two mechanisms from adjacent literature explain why AI changes the balance. Shaw and Nave (2026) describe cognitive surrender, the adoption of AI output with minimal scrutiny, and report that it inflates confidence even when the AI is wrong. Storey warns against the automation of understanding: using AI to generate the artifacts of comprehension without the cognitive work, which makes cognitive debt harder to detect.

Five observations hold across these designs.

  1. Individual gains are real but conditional. Bounded, well-specified tasks and less experienced developers show large gains (Peng; Cui). Experts working in mature code they know well showed a loss (METR). The variable is how much unwritten context the task depends on.

  2. Gains do not reach the team on their own. Output per developer rises while review time, batch size, and instability rise with it (DORA; Faros). The bottleneck moves from writing to verification.

  3. Structure erodes quietly. Duplication rises and refactoring falls across very large samples (GitClear), and the security of generated code is not improving with model capability (Veracode). Code health, in turn, conditions how safely agents can change code (Borg).

  4. Understanding is the hidden cost. Delegating to AI lowered comprehension without a speed gain when learning (Shen and Tamkin); developers misjudge their own speed (METR) and their own correctness (Shaw and Nave); and trust is falling as use rises (Stack Overflow).

  5. Agents already contribute at scale, unevenly. Their pull requests are mostly accepted, but acceptance depends strongly on task type and says little about maintenance afterwards.

Each observation maps onto the model proposed later. The first motivates writing context down (the intent bound). The second motivates reserving human reading for intent and evidence (H1, H6). The third motivates machine-enforced thresholds and feedback (H4, H5). The fourth motivates treating understanding as a deliverable. The fifth motivates increments small enough to be judged.

Specification levels

Böckeler distinguishes three levels of spec-driven development: spec-first (the specification guides the first implementation and is then forgotten), spec-anchored (the specification persists and evolves with the code), and spec-as-source (the specification is the source and code is generated from it), and observes that the tools she examined were in practice spec-first (Böckeler). We use this classification as one of our comparison criteria.

The software engineering canon

To judge what agents change, we need a statement of what the discipline held to be fundamental before them. We take that statement from three kinds of source.

Textbooks and the body of knowledge. Pressman and Maxim (2020) define software engineering as a layered technology: a quality focus at the base, then process, methods, and tools, with a generic process framework of communication, planning, modelling, construction, and deployment. Sommerville (2016) reduces every process to four fundamental activities: specification, development, validation, and evolution. The SWEBOK Guide, in its version 4.0, organises the discipline into eighteen knowledge areas and adds software architecture, operations, and security as areas in their own right (Washizaki, 2024).

Essays on the nature of the work. Brooks (1987) separates the essence of software, the conceptual construct of interlocking data, relationships, algorithms, and invocations, from the accidents of representing it, and argues that no tool that attacks only the accidents can yield an order-of-magnitude gain. Parnas (1972) grounds modular design in hiding decisions likely to change. Lehman (1980) states that a system in use must evolve and that its structure degrades as it does. Boehm (1981) documents the rising cost of change through the life cycle. Naur (1985), as discussed above, locates the program in the theory held by its builders.

The agile authors. The Agile Manifesto (Beck et al., 2001) states four value preferences and twelve principles, and its signatories wrote much of the practice literature that followed. Beck (1999, 2002) contributed Extreme Programming and test-driven development, built on the premise that the cost-of-change curve can be flattened by short feedback loops. Fowler (1999, 2018) made refactoring a named discipline and argued for evolutionary design and continuous integration (Fowler, 2004, 2006). Martin (2002, 2008, 2017) formulated the SOLID principles, the craft of readable code, and the dependency rule of Clean Architecture. Cockburn (2006) described development as a cooperative game of invention and communication. Hunt and Thomas (1999) introduced tracer bullets, thin end-to-end slices that prove an architecture early. Humble and Farley (2010) extended continuous integration into continuous delivery. Mellor and Balcer (2002) pursued executable models, an early form of specification as source.

Section 8 uses this canon as the baseline against which the Bounded Delegation hypothesis is contrasted.

The talks converge on four points, each consistent with the controlled evidence of Section 2.2.

  1. Verification, not generation, is the constraint. Karpathy, Farley, and Cherny say this in almost the same words, from three different positions.

  2. Human effort moves upstream. Horthy places review on research and plans; Grove on the specification; Farley on the acceptance test. None places it on the generated code.

  3. Context is a finite engineering resource. Horthy and Böckeler both treat what the agent reads as something to be designed, compacted, and isolated.

  4. What is learned must be written back. Cherny’s practice of updating the shared instruction file at review time is a small, working instance of a feedback loop on knowledge.

They also disagree in two places that matter here. The first is the medium of the specification: Grove argues for durable prose specifications, Farley and Tornhill for executable ones. The model in Section 7 takes both, prose for rationale and executable scenarios for behaviour, and H2 tests whether the executable part earns its cost. The second is how much process survives better models: Cherny’s reversal on planning within six months is a warning that any prescribed step may be an artifact of current model limits. This is the main reason the model is stated as bounds that people own, not as steps that agents must follow.

Agile requirements engineering and boundary objects

The problem this paper treats as new has a twenty-year precedent. Agile requirements engineering faced it with user stories: short written statements of intent whose detail was deliberately left to conversation. Sporsem, Dingsøyr, and Stol (2026) synthesise 14 industry studies into five propositions: (P1) user stories build shared understanding between developers and users; (P2) small stories help developers cope with change; (P3) stating the “why” focuses work on user needs but adds cost; (P4) the conversations stories trigger can hamper the sense of productivity; and (P5) stories as recorded in writing degrade over time.

Three of these bear directly on coding agents. First, the understanding a story produces lives in the memory of the people who held the conversation, and a reminder of a conversation one did not attend holds little value (P1, drawing on Conboy, 2021). An agent is a permanent newcomer: it starts every session outside every conversation. Second, Jeffries’s (2001) triad of card, conversation, and confirmation already paired a short statement of intent with acceptance tests. The two bounds of Section 7 are, in this sense, the card and the confirmation, with the conversation moved to the interview step. The novelty claimed here is therefore narrower than it may appear: not the pairing itself, but making both ends readable by an agent, locking the confirmation, and verifying each end independently of its author. Third, written stories degrade because the team’s mental model moves on and nobody updates the text (P5), the same mechanism this paper calls intent debt.

The review also recalls an older split in requirements engineering. Abstractionists specify systems through formal models; contextualists hold that context can never be fully written down (Potts and Hsi, 1997). Agile methods sided with the contextualists. Spec-driven development swings back towards writing everything down, because an agent can read nothing else. The model proposed here does not settle the debate; it bounds it. What must be written is what the agent needs at the two bounds; the rest of the context stays a human practice (Section 7.7).

The theoretical lens is boundary object theory. A boundary object keeps a common identity across social worlds while being specific enough to be used within each (Star and Griesemer, 1989). Carlile (2002) distinguishes three knowledge boundaries: syntactic (a shared vocabulary is missing), semantic (the same words are read differently), and pragmatic (groups have different interests at stake and must transform their knowledge). Levina and Vaast (2005) separate designated boundary objects, meant to span a boundary but not used that way, from boundary objects in use. Sporsem et al. close by asking whether user stories can carry requirements across a new boundary, from people to language models. This paper takes up that question for the whole knowledge base of an agent-assisted project (Section 7.9).

Boundary object theory has already been applied to the artifacts this paper reviews. Pareto, Eriksson, and Ehnebom (2010) treated architectural descriptions as boundary objects between system and design engineers; Sedano, Ralph, and Péraire (2019) treated the product backlog as one; Wohlrab et al. (2019) studied engineering artifacts in large-scale agile systems engineering; and Matthies et al. (2023) found four categories of boundary objects in DevOps projects, among them requirements and deployed-system information. What agents change is one side of the boundary: a reader with no tacit knowledge, no memory between sessions, and no stake in the outcome. Beneath all of this lies Tiwana’s (2004) view of software development as knowledge integration, which he linked empirically to development performance.

Method

Review design

Most of the techniques of interest are documented in repositories, vendor documentation, practitioner essays, and blog posts rather than in peer-reviewed venues. We therefore follow the logic of a multivocal literature review, which admits grey literature alongside formal literature (Garousi, Felderer, and Mäntylä, 2019). The review is narrative rather than systematic: we did not run a registered search protocol, and we report this as a limitation in Section 10.

This choice has a cost that a stricter design avoids. Sporsem et al. (2026), reviewing a related topic, excluded grey literature because it receives no independent scrutiny, kept only studies with an adequate methods description, and retained a proposition only when at least three distinct sources supported it. That design is not available here, since most techniques reviewed have no peer-reviewed description at all. We mitigate the gap in three ways: each empirical table states the design and type of its sources (Section 2.2); claims that rest on a vendor or a single practitioner are labelled as such; and Section 7.1 states the evidential basis of each sub-hypothesis, so that conjectures are not read as findings.

Selection

An approach was included if it (a) is intended for software development with coding agents, (b) prescribes where knowledge about the system is stored or how it is verified, and (c) had public documentation as of November 2026. We included established architecture-as-code practices because several agent-oriented techniques build on them. This yielded eleven approaches in four families:

  • Specification kits: GitHub Spec Kit, Kiro, OpenSpec, Tessl.

  • Multi-agent and review methods: BMAD Method, AWS AI-DLC, Elephant-Goldfish Model (EGM), mattpocock/skills.

  • Knowledge bases and architecture as code: LLM Wiki and the Open Knowledge Format (OKF); C4, arc42, and ADRs.

  • Code as specification: Tornhill’s AI-first workflow.

Two open formats, AGENTS.md and Agent Skills (SKILL.md), are treated as shared infrastructure rather than as competing approaches, since most of the techniques are distributed through them.

Sources

For each approach we consulted primary sources where available (official documentation, repositories, authors’ essays) and independent secondary analyses otherwise. Sources are cited inline and listed by theme in the references. Where a claim rests on a vendor or on a single practitioner report, we say so.

We also consulted published transcripts and summaries of conference talks and interviews (Section 2.5). We did not view the recordings; claims are taken from the transcripts and are treated as expert opinion.

Comparison framework

We compare the approaches on nine criteria. The first six describe how a technique works; the last three describe what it leaves out.

  1. Unit of knowledge. What counts as a document: a feature, a change, a capability, a phase, a code directory, a concept, a test.

  2. Directory structure. Where files live, how they are navigated, and whether current truth is separated from history.

  3. Cycle. The phases and the human approval gates.

  4. Agent roles and context. Whether agents are specialised and how much context each receives.

  5. Verification. How an incomplete, ambiguous, or wrong artifact is detected before it becomes code.

  6. Drift control. What happens after delivery.

  7. Specification level. Spec-first, spec-anchored, or spec-as-source (Section 2.3).

  8. Explicit architecture. Whether a model of components, relations, and decisions exists across features.

  9. Debts addressed. Technical, cognitive, and intent debt (Section 2.1).

Assessments on criteria 7–9 are the author’s qualitative judgement, based on the documented behaviour of each technique.

Results I: the approaches

For each approach we describe how it works and where it stores knowledge, then state its strengths and weaknesses.

Specification kits

GitHub Spec Kit.

Spec Kit is GitHub’s open-source kit. An initialisation command creates the structure, and a sequence of agent commands produces, in order, a constitution, a specification, a plan, tasks, and code (Microsoft Learn). The constitution holds principles that govern all decisions; each feature gets a numbered folder.

.specify/
|-- memory/constitution.md   # project principles
|-- templates/  scripts/
specs/
`-- 001-feature-name/
    |-- spec.md  plan.md  tasks.md
    `-- research.md  data-model.md

Strengths. Low adoption cost and support for many agents. A clarification step forces the agent to ask before planning, and an analysis step checks consistency across specification, plan, and tasks. Templates give a team a common format from day one.

Weaknesses. Each feature produces several long Markdown files, and reviewing them is more tiring than reviewing code, which invites rubber-stamping. Numbered folders accumulate with nothing to consolidate them: after thirty features there is no place to read current behaviour. The full flow is disproportionate for small fixes. Böckeler found it spec-first in practice and noted that agents do not always follow the constitution.

Agile requirements engineering recorded the same reflex. When developers found a user story unclear, the product owner answered with “a twenty-sentence specification”, which suppressed the conversation the story was meant to start (Ananjeva et al., 2020, as reviewed by Sporsem et al., 2026). Spec kits make that answer the default: each gap is closed with more text rather than with a conversation.

Kiro.

Kiro, AWS’s IDE, splits knowledge into two levels. Steering holds what applies to the whole project (product, stack, folder structure, conventions), and each steering file can declare when it is loaded into context. Specs hold each feature in three files: requirements in EARS notation (“WHEN … THE SYSTEM SHALL …”), a technical design, and tasks (DEV Community).

.kiro/
|-- steering/
|   |-- product.md  tech.md  structure.md
`-- specs/<feature>/
    |-- requirements.md  design.md  tasks.md

Strengths. Steering solves a real problem: the agent stops forgetting stack, conventions, and constraints each session, and inclusion modes avoid loading everything always. EARS makes each requirement testable. As an integrated environment, the flow does not depend on manual discipline.

Weaknesses. The flow is the same for any problem size; Böckeler reports a small bug expanding into several user stories with acceptance criteria. structure.md describes folders, not architecture, and there is no decision record. A practitioner report shows the consequence: with specs developed separately, inconsistencies surfaced only as the project grew (Stella Coding). The technique lives inside one vendor’s product, and nothing compares specification and code after delivery.

OpenSpec.

OpenSpec, from Fission AI, introduces the cleanest separation in this family: current system state and proposed changes live in different places. openspec/specs/ holds one specification per capability describing how the system works today. openspec/changes/ holds one folder per change with a proposal, design, tasks, and delta specs marking requirements as added, modified, or removed. Archiving a change merges its deltas into the canonical specs and moves the folder to a dated archive (OpenSpec).

openspec/
|-- specs/<capability>/spec.md       # current truth
`-- changes/
    |-- <change>/
    |   |-- proposal.md  design.md  tasks.md
    |   `-- specs/<capability>/spec.md   # deltas
    `-- archive/YYYY-MM-DD-<change>/

Strengths. Deltas focus human review on what changes. The specs folder is a living picture of the system that grows with each archived change, which suits existing systems. It is lightweight, validates format through a CLI, and is agent-agnostic. It is the only kit we classify as spec-anchored.

Weaknesses. Delta merging is the fragile point; the project’s repository carries fixes for specs falling out of sync on archive (OpenSpec PR 1241). The design.md, which holds technical rationale, belongs to the change and is archived with it, so the why is buried by date rather than linked to what it explains. Capabilities are functional units defined case by case: OpenSpec knows what the system does, not how it is divided or why.

Tessl.

Tessl holds the most ambitious vision: spec-as-source, where code is a regenerable artifact. Its most concrete contribution is a registry, which began with over 10,000 usage specs for open-source libraries and was repositioned in January 2026 as a registry of versioned, evaluated skills (codemyspec; Tessl).

Strengths. The registry targets a concrete and measurable problem, agents inventing APIs or mixing library versions, and treats knowledge with package-manager practices: versions, evaluations, and since 2026 a security score per skill (rywalker).

Weaknesses. The platform is proprietary and has changed positioning quickly. According to the same independent analysis, code regeneration from specs remains in closed beta and limited to JavaScript. Regeneration also meets model non-determinism: the same specification can yield different implementations, which moves the maintenance problem from code to sufficiently precise specifications, a familiar difficulty of model-driven development.

Multi-agent and review methods

BMAD Method.

BMAD reproduces an agile team with specialised agents (analyst, product manager, architect, product owner, scrum master, developer, QA) coordinated by an orchestrator. The analyst produces a brief, the product manager a PRD with epics, the architect an architecture document; the product owner validates them, and the scrum master shards the documents and drafts self-contained stories that the developer implements one at a time (BMAD).

docs/
|-- prd.md  architecture.md     # master documents
|-- prd/epic-1.md ...           # sharded by epic
|-- architecture/tech-stack.md ...
`-- stories/1.1-login.md ...    # one story per file

Strengths. It covers the part of the cycle the others ignore, product discovery before any specification. Stories carry the full context the developer agent needs, with source references. It is the only reviewed technique with a first-class architecture document and an architect role, and sharding addresses context size.

Weaknesses. It recreates, with agents, the hand-off chain of a waterfall process, and each hand-off is a chance of loss. Text volume is large and costly in tokens; its own guide recommends producing the large documents outside the IDE to save cost. Roles mirror human job titles with no evidence that this division helps a model; Horthy makes the same criticism from practice, arguing that subagents are useful for isolating context, not for imitating roles. Architecture is narrative, not a verifiable model. Pocock cites the method as an example of a framework that takes over a team’s process.

AI-DLC (AWS).

AI-DLC is AWS’s methodology for agent-assisted development, published as Markdown rules. It organises work by phase (inception, construction, operations), tracks workflow state, and keeps a complete audit trail. Every stage waits for explicit approval, and application code stays outside the documentation folder (skillselion). Recent versions organise each intent in its own folder with state, audit shards, and per-phase artifacts (AWS Labs).

aidlc-docs/
|-- inception/      requirements/ user-stories/ reverse-engineering/
|-- construction/   <unit>/ build-and-test/
|-- operations/
|-- aidlc-state.md  # where the workflow is
`-- audit.md        # every human input, timestamped

Strengths. The workflow is adaptive: stages are included or skipped by complexity. Questions to the user use a fixed multiple-choice format, which makes answers auditable. Sessions resume from state. Reverse engineering is a first-class stage, making it one of the few methods designed for existing systems. Its short cycles, called bolts, replace sprints.

Weaknesses. Explicit approval at every stage protects against a runaway agent, but repeated dozens of times it becomes reflex, the setting for cognitive surrender. Artifact volume per intent is high, and current knowledge about a component ends up scattered across folders of completed intents. Organisation by phase is good for auditing a workflow and poor for answering what the system is today.

Elephant-Goldfish Model (Google).

EGM starts from a deliberate asymmetry. Elephants, deep-context agents, run brainstorming and draft the design document. Goldfish, zero-context subagents, review the draft from the text and live code alone, without conversation bias (EGM). Review has five stages (zoom-out, comprehension, critic, a second zoom-out, implementation readiness). The method recommends that designs still go through human review by pull request. One skill compares code with specifications, with a recommendation to run it weekly; another generates bottom-up documentation for an existing codebase (getting started).

_agents/skills/<project>_elephant/SKILL.md   # loads context
docs/designs/
|-- SITEMAP.md      # index of design artifacts
`-- FEATURE.md      # detailed specification
src/**/README.md    # per-directory contract (bootstrap)

Strengths. Isolated review is the most original idea in the market: it tests whether a document stands on its own. Reviewer instructions forbid praise and default agreement, and each stage allows at most one re-audit so that review converges. Findings go to files, not chat. The drift report and bootstrap address two problems the kits ignore: what happens afterwards, and legacy code. The method states that it is not prescriptive and advises against a design process for a simple bug.

Weaknesses. Review produces a large amount of feedback by design, and the human must filter it. It requires a harness that can spawn isolated subagents and multiplies cost per document. There is no decision record as a unit and no architecture model above the design documents. All verification is performed on text: EGM checks that a specification is clear and complete, not that the described behaviour is the desired one.

mattpocock/skills.

Matt Pocock’s skills repository is deliberately the opposite of a method: small, composable skills adopted one at a time (GitHub). The most relevant here are the interview skills (grilling), which maintain the domain language in CONTEXT.md and decisions in ADRs, and those that turn a conversation into a specification, a specification into tickets, and tickets into code with TDD.

Strengths. Skills are small, readable, and impose no process. Grilling is the practice in this review that most directly counters cognitive debt: it makes the person think and answer rather than approve. The emphasis on TDD and deep modules keeps code in a state agents can modify.

Weaknesses. With no persistent specification, the intent of each feature dissolves into tickets and code after delivery; what remains is whatever reached CONTEXT.md and the ADRs. System structure stays implicit. As a toolkit rather than a method, results depend on the discipline of whoever combines the skills.

Knowledge bases and architecture as code

LLM Wiki and the Open Knowledge Format.

The LLM Wiki pattern, published by Andrej Karpathy in 2026, proposes that a model should not query raw documents on each question but compile a persistent wiki from them. It has three layers (immutable raw sources, a model-maintained wiki, and a schema file with conventions) and three operations (ingest, query, lint) (DenserAI). An index.md serves as a catalogue and a log.md records what happened in order. Google Cloud’s Open Knowledge Format formalised the pattern: Markdown with YAML frontmatter, where only type is required, linked by ordinary links into a graph.

Strengths. The format is as simple as possible (text files, links, a catalogue, a log) and so creates no tool lock-in. Knowledge compounds: each new source updates several pages rather than being reprocessed per question. OKF’s permissive reading tolerates unknown types and broken links, which allows gradual adoption.

Weaknesses. A model-maintained wiki can accumulate errors as naturally as knowledge, and linting finds internal inconsistencies, not errors against reality. Because the model writes and people only read, the pattern is a textbook case of automated understanding. It is generic and says nothing about the development cycle. OKF is at version 0.1.

C4, arc42, and ADRs.

Architecture as code predates agents. Simon Brown’s C4 model describes a system at four zoom levels (context, containers, components, code); written as a DSL in Structurizr or LikeC4, it yields several views from one model, and Structurizr’s documentation already describes agents generating the model from code and detecting drift (Structurizr). arc42 offers a twelve-section template for narrative documentation. Nygard’s ADRs record each significant decision with context and consequences.

Strengths. These are mature practices with stable tooling. A model in a DSL can be compared with code by machine, which no narrative document allows. An ADR is the cheapest intent artifact available: one page, written once, at the moment of decision.

Weaknesses. Their history is one of documents that go stale, because maintenance always depended on manual discipline. C4 describes structure, not behaviour. An ADR records a decision but nothing ensures the code continues to honour it. None of these practices has a development cycle with agents: they describe, but do not say when or by whom the description is written, reviewed, and updated.

Code as specification

Tornhill’s AI-first workflow.

Adam Tornhill argues for an inversion that cuts across the previous families. The old principle that programs are written for people to read, and only incidentally for machines to execute, no longer holds: code is now read mostly by machines. Yet he argues that abandoning code as a communication medium discards its most valuable property. Natural language, in his view, is the wrong level of abstraction for specification, and he cites Dave Farley: spec-driven development has the right diagnosis and the wrong medium (Tornhill).

The workflow has five moves:

  1. Purpose and constraints in prose. The change starts with motivation and known constraints.

  2. Expected outcome as end-to-end tests. Once the plan is scoped, the result is captured in system-level tests. They are part of the plan as much as the prose, and the human drives them, because they are a design and specification tool, not only a verification tool.

  3. Make the tests inspectable. Tornhill reports spending a disproportionate share of time here: defining the domain language, custom assertions, and where warranted a small DSL. The criterion is that a domain expert can decide from the tests alone whether the expected outcome is correct, without reading the implementation.

  4. Lock the suite. Tests are committed before implementation to guard against a known agent failure mode: making a test pass by altering the asserted data or deleting the failing test.

  5. Agents implement, with a loop back to prose. When an edge case teaches something, the human refines the written specification.

The difference between a generated test and an inspectable one can be illustrated with an appointment-rescheduling example. A typical agent-generated test:

@Test fun reschedule() {
  val r = http.post("/appointments/42/reschedule", """{"slotId": 7}""")
  assertEquals(200, r.status)
  assertEquals("TAKEN", db.query("select status from slot where id=7"))
  assertEquals(1, db.count("outbox where type='AppointmentRescheduled'"))
}

And an inspectable version that a receptionist could check:

@Test fun `patient reschedules and the old slot becomes free`() {
  val appointment = appointmentBooked(at = slot("Tue 9am"))

  val result = patient.reschedules(appointment, to = slot("Thu 2pm"))

  result.shouldSucceed()
  slot("Tue 9am").shouldBeFree()
  slot("Thu 2pm").shouldBeTakenBy(appointment)
  reminders.shouldAnnounceRescheduling(appointment)
}

Around this core Tornhill describes an automated discipline stricter than what he maintained by hand: 100% code coverage as the default bar, end-to-end tests for every feature, a high code-quality threshold, and several independent automated code reviews per change. This automation lets him iterate at system level and rarely read the implementation. He closes with an argument about completeness: a pipeline of gates gives guarantees about what exists and none about what is missing, and those who can read executable evidence are better placed to notice the gap.

Strengths. The specification stops being a promise and becomes evidence: the test passes or it does not. Code is compact and unambiguous, which shortens re-reading at each iteration. Human friction is concentrated where it pays most, in deciding what correct behaviour is, which counters cognitive debt at the boundary without requiring the human to read everything. The locked suite protects intent against the agent itself.

Weaknesses. It requires strong programming skill, which Tornhill acknowledges excludes those who specify without coding. Tests state what the system does, not why it was decided or how it is divided: they replace neither decision records nor an architecture model. End-to-end tests are slow and brittle in large systems. A reader’s question on the article remains only partly answered: if the agent writes both the implementation and the lower-level tests, those tests may only confirm the agent’s own understanding. Rarely reading the implementation is a bet that holds while quality automation is trustworthy. Finally, it is one experienced developer’s workflow, not a team method.

Three findings follow.

Finding 1 (RQ1): intent is captured at delivery but rarely kept. Almost every technique addresses intent debt at the moment of delivery. Only OpenSpec, EGM, and Tornhill’s workflow have a mechanism that prevents it from returning.

Finding 2 (RQ2): cognitive debt is a blind spot. Where it appears at all, it is a side effect of a human gate, not an objective. No technique measures it.

The gap is not new. In agile requirements engineering, user stories were adopted precisely to build shared understanding through conversation, and the evidence shows that this understanding is lost when the people who held the conversations leave (Sporsem et al., 2026, P1). The agent techniques reviewed here inherit the written half of that practice and drop the conversational half.

Finding 3 (RQ1, RQ2): verification is split in two. Prose-based techniques check that a text is clear, complete, and coherent. Tornhill’s workflow checks that behaviour is the desired one, but keeps neither rationale nor structure. No reviewed technique does both, and none maintains an explicit architecture model across features together with a development cycle.

The upper-right region is empty. Techniques that verify well operate at feature scope; those that organise system-level or organisation-level knowledge barely verify. The model proposed in Section 7 targets that region.

The EGM structure

EGM combines two axes. Design documents live in docs/designs/, indexed by a sitemap. The more original idea is in its bootstrap skill, which the project calls “peanuts and hay”. Peanuts are README files in leaf directories holding per-file contracts: interfaces, invariants, and side effects. Hay is the set of parent READMEs that roll child contracts up into progressively higher-altitude indexes, until the root becomes a map of the system’s architecture (egm_bootstrap). Generated content sits inside sentinel blocks so that human-written text is preserved.

Five properties make this structure especially relevant to agent-assisted work:

  1. Co-location. Knowledge about a directory lives in that directory. An agent opening the code finds the contract without searching, and a change to code and contract fits in one pull request.

  2. Emergent zoom. The README hierarchy reproduces the zoom levels of C4 without anyone drawing them: leaf as component, intermediate as container, root as system.

  3. Contracts, not descriptions. Interfaces, invariants, and side effects are the knowledge most useful for implementation and easiest to verify against code.

  4. Provenance. Sentinel blocks separate machine-generated from human-written content.

  5. Checkable by a goldfish. Because everything is linked by files, a zero-context subagent can navigate the hierarchy; what it cannot reach is not written down.

Where it falls short

The physical folder tree is not the logical architecture: cross-cutting concerns, components spread across folders, and systems split across repositories do not fit a directory hierarchy. Contracts say what, not why; decisions and discarded alternatives have no place in a component README. And automatic bottom-up generation runs exactly the risk Storey calls automation of understanding; EGM itself notes that humans still need to review the output.

Finding 4 (RQ1): no single axis suffices. The strongest structure combines four: current truth separated from changes (OpenSpec), contracts co-located with code (EGM), a logical model that cuts across folders (C4), and a linked base of concepts and decisions (OKF).

The weakness of the by-feature axis has an empirical precedent. In large-scale agile programmes, teams working from some 2,000 user stories struggled to describe the complete system and fell back on epics and themes to regain an overview (Kasauli et al., 2021, as reviewed by Sporsem et al., 2026). Feature-scoped specification kits reproduce the same structure, at agent speed.

The Bounded Delegation model

This section answers RQ3. The four findings point to the same gap: techniques either bound the agent’s work with prose that nobody can execute, or with tests that carry no rationale, and none keeps people’s understanding in the loop by design. The model below combines the strongest element of each reviewed approach around the central hypothesis H0 (Section 1.2).

Sub-hypotheses

H0 decomposes into six sub-hypotheses. Each states a comparison and a predicted direction, so that each can fail independently.

Sub-hypotheses of the Bounded Delegation model.
ID Hypothesis Prediction Derived from
H1 Boundary reading. Humans who read intent and inspectable scenarios, and read implementation only by exception, detect behavioural defects at least as well as humans who review implementation line by line. Equal or lower escaped-defect rate, with lower human review time per change Tornhill; DORA on verification cost
H2 Executable bound. Locked, inspectable scenarios written before implementation reduce behaviour drift relative to prose specifications. Fewer divergences between stakeholder expectation and observed behaviour Tornhill; Farley; Finding 3; Carlile (semantic boundary)
H3 Context-free verification. Artifacts that pass zero-context review require fewer agent clarifications and less rework than artifacts reviewed by context-rich reviewers. Fewer clarification requests and fewer tokens per change EGM; Storey on intent debt

H5 Feedback. Per-layer feedback from evidence back to intent slows the growth of artifact staleness and model-code drift. Lower drift and artifact age over 6 to 12 months OpenSpec; Lehman; Finding 1; Sporsem et al. (P5)

If H1 fails, humans must keep reading implementation and the model’s economy collapses. If H3 fails, isolated review is cost without benefit. The hypotheses are deliberately separable.

The evidential basis is uneven, and we state it plainly. By the threshold Sporsem et al. (2026) apply, at least three distinct empirical sources per proposition, none of the six qualifies yet. H1, H5, and H6 draw on at least one peer-reviewed or large-survey source (DORA; Lehman), while H2, H3, and H4 rest mainly on practitioner reports and on this review’s own findings. All six are therefore conjectures to be tested, not propositions synthesised from evidence.

Seven knowledge layers

The model organises knowledge in seven layers. Layers 1–3 form the intent bound; layer 7 is the evidence bound; layers 4–6 are the delegated path.

  1. Problem. Who is affected, what counts as success, constraints, and quality scenarios. Humans decide.

  2. Domain. The business language, term by term. Humans decide.

  3. Decisions. ADRs with machine-checkable assertions. Humans decide.

  4. Structure. A C4 model linked to component contracts in the code.

  5. Patterns. Recurring solutions with implementation steps.

  6. Steps. Changes in progress, with deltas over the layers above.

  7. Evidence. Inspectable executable scenarios, architecture tests, and evaluations. Humans read; agents may not edit.

Layer 7 is not written last. Executable scenarios are drafted right after the problem, before any step, and they, rather than a prose document, are the specification the agent must satisfy. The prose of layers 1–3 keeps what tests cannot say: the rationale.

Rationale has a cost, and the model must place it where it is paid once. Stating the “why” focuses work on user needs, but the true reason often takes several rounds of asking, and developers omitted it when the same reason applied to many stories (Sporsem et al., 2026, P3; Diebold et al., 2018). The layering answers this: a reason that holds for many increments is written once, in layer 1 or 3, and each increment links to it instead of restating it. Technical requirements with no user to speak for them go to decisions, not to scenarios, much as Kniberg (2015) kept tech stories apart from user stories.

Scenarios alone are not a specification. Meyer’s (2018) criticism of user stories applies to them unchanged: piling up case over case does not give a specification, only a system that handles the planned cases and possibly nothing else. This is why layer 7 is bounded from above by layers 1–4: quality scenarios, decisions, and structure state the general properties that no list of cases implies. Non-functional requirements are the clearest gap. User stories handled them poorly (Behutiye et al., 2020), and end-to-end scenarios inherit that weakness unless the quality scenarios of layer 1 are turned into checks.

Three roles

From EGM the model takes its second rule: authors do not verify their own artifacts. Elephants receive maximal context and draft. Goldfish receive none: only the artifact, the files it links to, and the code. Humans decide the premises in layers 1–3 and, before approving, explain the change in their own words. The goldfish measures intent debt (is the artifact self-sufficient?); the human explanation measures cognitive debt (does the team understand it?). Neither test replaces the other.

The human role has a name in boundary object theory. Levina and Vaast (2005) call boundary spanners the people who adapt objects to each side, show others how to use them, and introduce them at the right time. In agile requirements engineering, a story’s effect depended more on the skill of the person writing and telling it than on the story itself (Sporsem et al., 2026, P1; Conboy, 2021). Under bounded delegation, keeping the bounds legible to both people and agents is that spanning work: a skill to be valued when staffing a team, not a by-product of approving changes.

The iterative and incremental cycle

Each increment is a thin, complete slice of behaviour, small enough to be understood at once and delivered in hours or a few days. The cycle alternates between humans and agents, and humans appear at four bounds.

  1. Intent. A person states the problem, the motivation, and known constraints.

  2. Interview. An elephant with system context asks until gaps close; new terms go to the glossary, and a structural decision becomes a draft ADR.

  3. Executable scenarios. The person drives, with the agent, the end-to-end tests of the increment, makes them readable in domain language, and commits the suite. From here, implementing agents cannot change it.

  4. Isolated review. Goldfish read the scenarios and the layer deltas without the conversation and report what does not stand alone.

  5. Gate. The person approves what is premise and explains the change back.

  6. Implementation. Agents implement in small batches, each in an ephemeral workspace, integrating to trunk behind a feature flag when the increment is incomplete.

  7. Verification. The pipeline runs the locked scenarios, the architecture tests, automated reviews, and model-code comparison. Failures return to step 6 without human involvement.

  8. Learning. The person reads results (scenarios, production metrics, feedback signals) and adjusts the intent of the next increment.

The evidence on user stories supports this size. Small stories were estimated more accurately, exposed dependencies earlier, and shortened the wait for user feedback; large ones grew unexpectedly and produced more code than needed (Liskin et al., 2014, as reviewed by Sporsem et al., 2026, P2). Small items also gave developers short-term certainty inside long-term uncertainty, which Vidgen and Wang (2009) relate to Baskerville’s (2006) “artful planning”. The same review warns that the benefit fades at scale, where many small items must be regrouped to keep an overview; in the model that regrouping belongs to layers 1 and 4, not to the increment.

The cycle formalises what practitioners already report doing. Karpathy describes the same alternation as a generation-verification loop to be spun as fast as possible in small chunks. Horthy places the human checkpoints on research and plans rather than on code. Cherny names the agent’s ability to verify its own work as the practice that matters most. The model adds two things those accounts leave implicit: that the verification artifact is owned and locked by a person, and that the reviewer of each artifact starts without context.

The feedback ring

Each layer returns a signal to the one above when reality contradicts it: a failing test creates a new step; repeated steps become a pattern; a pattern that does not fit changes the model; recurring model-code drift reopens a decision; a decision that meets an ambiguous term returns to the glossary; the domain may show that the problem was a different one. The top layer closes the ring by returning new scenarios to the evidence layer. Returns slow down as they rise, so that the system does not oscillate, and each leaves a line in log.md.

Not every artifact must be kept current. Sporsem et al. (2026, P5) find that written user stories degrade once implemented and ask whether this is a problem at all, since the story has served its purpose. The model accepts that answer for layer 6: a change folder is a reminder of a conversation, and it is archived rather than maintained. It rejects it for layers 1–5 and 7, which agents read as current truth in every session. A stale story misleads one newcomer once; a stale contract misleads every agent run. The ring therefore applies only to the layers that are read as current.

Alongside artifact sensors, the ring has sensors for cognitive debt, taken from Storey’s diagnostic signals: changes with unexpected results, components nobody wants to touch, knowledge concentrated in one person. The response to these is never a generated document. It is a human practice: an explanation, a walkthrough, a pairing session.

Agile teams already had such a practice. In a longitudinal case, developers working in rotating pairs spread their understanding of user needs well beyond what the written stories held (Vidgen and Wang, 2009). Rotation can survive when agents write the code: the pair’s task becomes reading the bounds together and explaining a change to each other.

Code that both readers understand

If humans read implementation only by exception, do Clean Code and Clean Architecture still matter? The principles matter more; the ceremony matters less. Borg et al. (2026) found an association between code health, a metric calibrated for human comprehension, and behaviour preservation after AI refactoring, though on small, isolated files.

The criterion for any abstraction becomes a single question: does it reduce what a human and an agent must read to change something safely?

The bounds as boundary objects

Read through boundary object theory (Section 2.6), the model’s human-owned layers are boundary objects between people and agents, and each addresses one of Carlile’s (2002) knowledge boundaries (table below).

Carlile’s knowledge boundaries between people and agents.
Boundary What goes wrong between people and agents Layer that carries it Check in the model
Syntactic The agent uses words the team does not use, or invents names 2. Domain (glossary) Names in domain language; goldfish flag undefined terms
Semantic The agent reads the same words differently from the team 7. Evidence (executable scenarios) A scenario passes or fails; a second reading cannot pass silently
Pragmatic Interests conflict: speed against structure, one team’s change against another’s 3. Decisions (ADRs) and the human gate People decide; agents propose a revision when a signal repeats

Two consequences follow. First, an executable scenario crosses the semantic boundary in a way a user story could not. The story left meaning to a later conversation; a scenario fixes it in a form that a person and an agent check the same way. This is the strongest theoretical reason for H2. Second, the pragmatic boundary cannot be delegated. Carlile holds that crossing it requires the parties to transform their knowledge and to be able to alter the object; an agent can propose, but the trade-off between interests stays with people. This is why layers 1–3 are human-owned.

Levina and Vaast’s (2005) distinction gives a test for the gate. An approved artifact that nobody reads again is a designated boundary object, not one in use, and repeated approval without reading (Section 4.2, AI-DLC) produces exactly that. The explain-back at the gate and the goldfish review exist to keep the bounds in use. H5’s measure of artifact age is, in these terms, a measure of how long a bound stays in use. Sporsem et al.’s (2026) open questions on how people and language models read the same requirement at each boundary map onto H2 (semantic) and H3 (syntactic) of this agenda.

The theory also names a tension the model must hold. Crossing a pragmatic boundary requires interpretive flexibility: an object each group can read from its own interests while it stays a common reference (Star, 2010; Doolin and McLeod, 2012). An executable scenario removes that flexibility by design. The model therefore keeps the two properties in different layers: the prose of layers 1–3 stays open to negotiation; the scenarios of layer 7 are closed to implementing agents from step 3 of the cycle (Section 7.4) but stay open to people until the gate at step 5, where the negotiation ends. Treating a scenario as final before the pragmatic boundary is crossed would turn one group’s reading into everyone’s constraint. Boundary objects are also in use only for periods (Sporsem et al., 2026): a bound that people have stopped reading has become a designated object again, whatever its age.

Contrast with the software engineering canon

This section answers RQ4 by contrasting the model with the traditional literature. It proceeds from the general to the specific: the fundamentals stated by the textbooks and the body of knowledge (8.1), the values of the Agile Manifesto (8.2), the current positions of its authors (8.3), the process practices built on those foundations (8.4), and the recommendations that follow (8.5). The overall result is that the foundations hold and their allocation changes.

Fundamentals preserved and fundamentals that change

Read against the canon of Section 2.4, bounded delegation is conservative about what software engineering is and radical about who does which part of it. Table 3 contrasts each fundamental with its fate under the hypothesis.

Three observations follow from the table.

First, the hypothesis is Brooks’s argument restated for a new tool. Brooks predicted that attacking accidental complexity would never deliver an order-of-magnitude gain in the whole, because the essence would remain. Agents are the most powerful attack on accident so far, and the empirical record of Section 2.2, with large individual speed-ups and flat team outcomes, is what Brooks’s argument predicts. The two bounds of the model are the essence in his sense: deciding what the construct should be, and establishing that the construct built is the one intended.

Second, Sommerville’s four activities survive intact, and this is the strongest evidence that the discipline’s foundations are not being replaced. What changes is their allocation. In the textbook account one team performs all four. Under bounded delegation, people hold specification and validation, agents hold development, and evolution is a loop between them.

Third, the single fundamental that changes in kind is the readership of code. The canon’s craft literature, from Martin’s Clean Code to Fowler’s Refactoring, assumes that the primary reader of a program is another programmer. Section 7.8 argued that the practices this literature recommends still pay off, because code that is healthy for people is also safer for agents to change. But the reason shifts, from courtesy to a future colleague to a property that machines can measure and enforce.

The Agile Manifesto revisited

The Manifesto (Beck et al., 2001) is often read as a rejection of documentation and process. Its text is a set of preferences, and each can be re-read with agents as the main implementers.

Value Original concern Reading under bounded delegation Verdict
Individuals and interactions over processes and tools Heavy process displaced the judgement of the people doing the work The tool is now a participant. Interactions among people become the scarce resource, since that is where understanding is built; agent throughput must not crowd them out Preserved, with higher stakes

Several of the twelve principles shift in the same way. “Working software is the primary measure of progress” holds unchanged. “Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility” gains empirical support from the code-health evidence. “Simplicity, the art of maximising the amount of work not done” becomes more important, because an agent’s default is to produce more. “Sustainable pace” re-appears as a limit on pending human decisions (H6). The principle that face-to-face conversation is the most effective way to convey information needs qualification: it remains true among people, but an agent was not in the room, so the outcome of the conversation has to be written down before it can guide the work. Sedano, Ralph, and Péraire (2019) state the tension precisely: a good specification replaces face-to-face communication, while a good backlog facilitates it. Bounded delegation needs both. The bounds replace conversation for the agent, and must not replace it among people.

Cockburn’s (2006) description of development as a cooperative game of invention and communication survives well. The game now has a new kind of player, one that communicates only through written artifacts and remembers nothing between sessions. Much of the model in Section 7 is a response to that single property.

The Extreme Programming practices fare differently from one another. Test-driven development moves from a programmer’s technique to the contract between people and agents. Continuous integration and small releases become preconditions rather than aspirations. Refactoring is delegated but not diminished. Pair programming changes most: pairing with an agent keeps the productivity benefit but loses the function Beck (1999) valued most, the spreading of knowledge between two people, which is why the model replaces it with explicit understanding practices rather than assuming it away. Hunt and Thomas’s (1999) tracer bullets, thin end-to-end slices that prove the architecture, are the increments of Section 7.4 almost unchanged.

What the canon’s authors say now

Three signatories of the Manifesto have published their own positions on working with agents, and they do not fully agree, which is informative for H1.

Beck distinguishes augmented coding from vibe coding. In the former the developer still cares about the code, its complexity, its tests, and their coverage; the value system is that of hand coding, with less typing. He reports abandoning two attempts at a project because accumulated complexity stalled the agent, and treats test-driven development as the way to keep it on course (Beck, 2025).

Fowler argues that LLMs change programming as much as the move from assembler to high-level languages did, with a difference: the new abstraction is non-deterministic, a sideways move rather than only an upward one (Fowler, 2025). He suggests that software engineering will need the notion of tolerances that other engineering disciplines use, and that rigorous testing and refactoring matter more, not less (The New Stack; The Pragmatic Engineer). He also notes that developers usually gain their understanding of a domain and a system in the act of building it, which is the mechanism behind cognitive debt (Fowler, 2026).

Martin, whose Clean Code taught a generation to write for human readers, reported in 2026 that he no longer reads the code his agents write. He surrounds them instead with constraints: unit tests, Gherkin acceptance tests, QA procedures, complexity and module-size thresholds, dependency analysis, mutation testing, and coverage (Startup Fortune; Noqta). Secondary reports add that he continues to draw module boundaries himself.

These positions map onto the model. Martin’s practice and Tornhill’s are, independently, the boundary-reading stance of H1: people own acceptance tests and structure, machines check everything else. Beck’s position keeps the human closer to the code. Fowler’s concern with non-determinism explains why the evidence bound must be executable and locked: a prose specification interpreted by a non-deterministic tool gives no stable guarantee. The disagreement between Beck and Martin is the open empirical question that H1 is designed to settle, and it is notable that it now divides authors who agreed on almost everything in 2001.

Practices that lose their rationale

If the fundamentals hold, why should practices change? Because much of the development process of the last two decades was designed to ration one scarce resource: programmer hours. When implementation takes minutes, the scarce resource becomes human attention for deciding, checking, and understanding. Practices that existed to manage the first lose their rationale, while the principles beneath them gain importance.

Four qualifications apply. First, obsolete does not mean wrong: these practices solved real problems, and the principles behind them (small batches, fast feedback, limited work in progress) become more important, not less. DORA’s research associates AI adoption with both higher throughput and higher instability and names small batches and strong version control among the capabilities that counter it (Google Cloud). Second, the evidence is young. Much of the debate on the end of sprints comes from opinion pieces (Jansen; doubleSlash), and there are experiments in the opposite direction, such as a working paper on 324 two-hour agent-run sprints which concludes that continuous integration and merging cost as much time as generation (Agent Scrum). Third, context matters: regulated organisations, hardware programmes, and fixed-date contracts will keep coordination cadences, and even there the emerging proposal is a dual rhythm, with execution in flow and coordination in cadence (Agility at Scale).

A fourth qualification concerns sprints. Their rationale was not only to ration programmer hours. Fixed iterations in which the stories did not change gave one team a steady pace, freedom from distraction, and relief from anxiety (Vidgen and Wang, 2009; Sporsem et al., 2026, P2). Continuous flow removes that protection from the people who decide and review. Whatever replaces the sprint should keep a bounded window in which a person’s queue of decisions does not change, which is what the limit on pending decisions in H6 is meant to provide.

What changes is the planning unit. It stops being the programmer hour and becomes the pending human decision. A team is overloaded when it has more scenarios to check, decisions to make, and signals to answer than it can understand, not when it has too many tasks in progress.

The following recommendations are conditional on the hypotheses of Section 7.1 and should be read as a strategy to be tested, not as established results.

Decision 8 deserves emphasis. DORA’s 2026 ROI report describes a J-curve in which productivity dips before rising, driven by learning and the cost of verifying AI output (InfoQ). A telemetry analysis attributed to LinearB, covering 8.1 million pull requests, reports that AI-generated code waits 4.6 times longer for a first review (Ingenire). DORA also warns against rewarding token consumption as a performance indicator (DORA). A strategy that only accelerates generation fills the review queue; one that works reduces what needs human eyes and makes the rest machine-verifiable.

Adoption can itself be incremental. A plausible sequence over four quarters is: foundations (trunk, fast pipeline, AGENTS.md, CONTEXT.md, first ADRs, a code-health threshold); the executable bound (human-driven locked scenarios, contracts on the most-changed components); verification and structure (isolated review, a C4 model to container level, architecture tests, drift reports); and finally the ring (per-layer sensors and thresholds, the feedback log, debt metrics, and retirement of ceremonies that lost their function).

Measuring the three debts

Testing H0 as a whole requires measures for all three debts, since a purely technical result could hide the effect of greatest concern.

  • Technical debt: code health scores, defect density, violated architecture assertions.

  • Intent debt: the share of quality scenarios and ADR assertions covered by a check; the comprehension rate of zero-context agents asked to explain a component from its artifacts alone; the distance between documented and observed behaviour.

  • Cognitive debt: onboarding time to a first unassisted approved change; knowledge concentration per component in version history; the ability of approvers to explain, unaided, a change they approved; the frequency of changes with unexpected results (Storey, 2026).

Agile requirements engineering offers baselines for intent debt. In 1,636 backlog items from 14 projects, 45.5% held two or more requirements and more than half gave no justification (Van Can and Dalpiaz, 2024, as reviewed by Sporsem et al., 2026). The same counts can be taken on increments under each condition of Section 9.2. A further question belongs to the pragmatic boundary and is not covered by H1 to H6: who holds the power to phrase the bounds, and how asymmetries between technical and non-technical stakeholders shape what an agent is told (Sporsem et al., 2026, Table 6).

A comparative experiment

A fair comparative study would implement the same 20 to 30 changes under four conditions: a spec-first kit (Spec Kit or Kiro), OpenSpec, EGM, and the full Bounded Delegation model, measuring the three debts and throughput. Given METR’s experience, in which a follow-up trial became hard to interpret because developers would no longer work without AI, the control condition should vary the method rather than the presence of AI.

Expected failure modes

We expect the model to perform poorly, relative to simpler alternatives, on small short-lived systems where the cost of maintaining seven layers exceeds its benefit; in domains where behaviour is hard to express as end-to-end tests, such as exploratory data work or highly visual interfaces; and in teams without the programming skill needed to judge inspectable tests. Reporting these boundary conditions is part of the agenda.

A fourth failure mode is abandonment. The model adds deliberate friction: interviews, explain-back at the gate, and isolated review. Agile requirements engineering shows where such friction can lead. Conversations triggered by user stories hampered developers’ sense of productivity (Sporsem et al., 2026, P4), and in one longitudinal case a team under a “get on with it” manager dropped user stories altogether (McAvoy and Butler, 2005). H6 measures stability, not adoption, so the evaluation should also record whether teams still follow the practices after six and twelve months.

Threats to validity

Construct validity. The three debts are recent constructs. Technical debt has established measures; cognitive and intent debt do not, and the measures proposed in Section 9.1 are untested proxies. The techniques themselves raise the same problem. User stories are operationalised so differently across companies that two may be distinct constructs (Sporsem et al., 2026), and “spec-driven development” likewise names practices that differ in unit, level, and verification. Assessments in Table 2 and the qualitative positioning described around Figure 1 are the author’s qualitative judgement, not measurements.

Internal validity. The review is narrative and was conducted by a single author without a registered protocol or independent coding, so selection and interpretation bias are possible. The model was developed by the same author who performed the comparison, which favours a reading in which the gaps found match the solution proposed. Two practices used by Sporsem et al. (2026) would reduce this bias in a next version: independent screening by a second researcher, and asking the maintainers of each technique to confirm its description, as they asked the authors of their primary studies to confirm study contexts.

External validity. Much of the evidence comes from vendor documentation, vendor-published telemetry, and practitioner essays. Tornhill’s workflow is a single expert’s account. EGM’s claims about use at scale come from its publisher. Martin’s current position is taken from secondary reports of his social-media posts, and the readings of the Manifesto in Table 7 are the author’s interpretation, not its signatories’. The talks in Section 2.5 are expert opinion, read from published transcripts and summaries rather than viewed, and several speakers work for companies that sell the tools they describe (Anthropic, OpenAI, HumanLayer). The empirical studies in Section 2.2 cover specific populations (experienced open-source maintainers, survey respondents, small Python files) and may not generalise to other settings. Unlike theoretical reviews restricted to peer-reviewed empirical studies (Sporsem et al., 2026), most sources here are grey literature, so the findings are closer to a map of claims than to a synthesis of evidence. Coverage of contexts limits external validity further. The user story literature is concentrated on business software in Europe and North America, with South America, Africa, and much of Asia under-represented (Sporsem et al., 2026). The evidence here has the same skew. Replications in under-represented settings, Brazil among them, would test whether the hypotheses travel.

Temporal validity. The field moves quickly. Tessl repositioned itself in January 2026, AI-DLC reorganised its folder structure between versions, and EGM was open-sourced weeks before this snapshot (November 2026). METR flagged its own 2025 result as dated within a year. Conclusions tied to specific tools may not hold for long; the comparison framework and the hypotheses are intended to outlast them.

Conclusion validity. No part of the Bounded Delegation model has been evaluated empirically. Sections 7 and 8 are hypotheses and conditional recommendations. The claim that certain practices “lose their rationale” is an argument from changed constraints, supported by early and partly anecdotal evidence, and is itself among the claims to be tested (H6).

Conclusion

We reviewed eleven approaches to agent-assisted development and compared them on nine criteria. On RQ1, the techniques converge on writing before coding, storing knowledge as Markdown in the repository, and approving at human gates, but diverge on the unit of knowledge, and that choice determines what each sees and what it loses. On RQ2, intent debt is widely addressed at delivery and rarely kept from returning; cognitive debt is a blind spot; and verification is split between checking prose and checking behaviour.

On RQ3, we proposed the Bounded Delegation hypothesis: delegation of implementation to agents is sustainable when bounded by human-owned intent on one side and human-readable executable evidence on the other, with both bounds verified independently and kept current by feedback. The model built on it takes current-truth separation from OpenSpec, co-located contracts and context-free review from EGM, inspectable executable scenarios from Tornhill, and an explicit architecture model and decision records from established practice. Its operational summary is one sentence: humans read intent and evidence; agents read and write the path between them. Read through boundary object theory, the two bounds are boundary objects between people and agents, and the model is a successor to the card and confirmation of agile requirements engineering, not a break from it.

On RQ4, the contrast with the canon shows more continuity than rupture. Brooks’s distinction between essence and accident, Sommerville’s four fundamental activities, Pressman’s quality focus, Parnas’s information hiding, and Lehman’s laws all hold; what changes is who performs which activity, and who reads the code. The Agile Manifesto’s values survive, with recorded intent no longer optional and executable scenarios serving as documentation that runs. Its authors now disagree on whether people should still read implementation, which is the question H1 puts to test. The practices designed to ration programmer hours (long-lived branches, time-boxed sprints, velocity estimation, hand-off boards, release trains) lose their rationale when the scarce resource becomes human attention, while the principles beneath them (small batches, fast feedback, limited work in progress) gain importance. One function of the sprint does not lose its rationale: the stability fixed iterations gave the people who decide, which the model must replace rather than drop.

None of this has been tested. The contribution of this paper is a framework for comparison, a hypothesis precise enough to be wrong, and an agenda for finding out. Naur’s thesis still stands behind it: what must be preserved is the theory of the system. What has changed is that losing it now costs more and happens faster.
